## Introduction

  A few years ago, when generative AI just came into most people's view, I encountered a Chinese reading comprehension passage which claimed that while AI could imitate literary styles, it could never produce writing of the same caliber — because it lacked the lived experience of the original author, Li Bai being the example given. The conclusion was unequivocal: AI would never replace human writers.  

  As the time goes by, the assertion that passage made became less and less convincing, however. With the iteration of models, ai is getting more and more competent in writing passages. Does the so called "lived experience" really matter? If something looks like a duck, acts like a duck, then it is probably a duck. The same law may apply to writing as well.  

  And if AI can write, what else can it do? That question led me somewhere I didn't expect — into my own experience of learning and doing research, and into an uncomfortable realization: the parts we're losing may be the parts that matter most.  

## Main text

  This is the first post of my blog, and the second of the whole website. What I want to discuss here is the phenomenon of using Artificial Intelligence in various areas, to be more precisely, in learning and scientific research.

### The origin

  The first AI agent I've ever known is ChatGPT. That was back in 2023, when I was a high school student. As the most powerful AI back then, though it showed impressive competence in dialogue, its limitations were also apparent. That is, AI hallucination, a problem that has yet to be solved. It made up things and events that actually didn't exist. And it got common sense wrong. And when it came to mathematical problems, it seldom gave the correct answer. I only considered it as a toy at that time, and I thought it would take at least a decade before it became capable enough to replace humans. Then I quickly forgot about it, as the college entrance examination was coming near.(And as a student of a senior school in a small county town, I had limited access to AI, both in time device.) I had no idea how fast things would change.  

### Phase 0

  A year went by, and I was in university. My first semester felt no different from before. I took notes in class, did my homework on my own, and looked things up online when I needed to learn more. Back then, I only used AI in liberal arts courses — writing and communication, and ideological & political education, to be more exact, and for information searching. I hate writing about things I don't like, even now. That's also why I hated Chinese class in high school. Honestly speaking, AI helped me a lot in this aspect, freeing me from spending hours and days producing rubbish painfully.  
  
### Phase 1

  Things changed a little bit, at the end of that semester. We were required to complete a project which was quite challenging for us at that time - to make a Tetris game using the given framework. All that we learned in class was only the basics of Classic C syntax and some fundamental data types. So, we had to learn more stuff ourselves, including rendering, event handling, to name but a few. Firstly I asked AI what I needed to learn to complete this job and where I could find related learning articles, then I checked whether the information it gave was right and learned it, implemented it for the first month starting from when the work was issued. Then I happened to come across Github Copilot, a coding agent made by microsoft. That's the very first AI I've ever used in programming. It could handle small projects quite well, but when the codebase got larger, it started losing track of how different parts fitted together and making up functions that did not exist, because models at that time did not have such a long context window. So I only used it for hints, for info searching, or for generating templates that I would adjust according to the project myself. Anyway, it did help me a lot, saving me plenty of time looking up related information and docs online, and thus accelerating the development of the project. And I earned an A+, the highest rank, for that course in the end. By the way, I have got A+ in only two classes till now(2026-09-23). At that time, I began to consider ai as a helpful assistant, but only for work that did not require much reasoning. After all, the **L** in L**L**M is for **Language**, rather than **Logic**.

  Then at the start of 2025, DeepSeek-R1(DS for short in the following passages) came out. I quickly fell in love with it, especially in handling some assignments that I did not like(Outline of Modern Chinese History, for example). Firstly I downloaded the ppt of the class online, then uploaded it to ds, copied the requirements of the work and pasted it in the dialogue box, clicked "send" and I got the original article. After about 1h's polishing, all the work was accomplished. All of these happened only in 2h.(Now I even do not need to review the article myself, so it is faster, costing about 20 min.) As for coding, I hoped I could learn something, so I disabled the copilot extension in vscode and did the work myself. I still remember the days I spent hours debugging the program back then - setting breakpoints, constructing test cases, stepping through the code line by line, which has become a luxury today. I only used ai when necessary: to get some hint after hours of trying, to read compiler or runtime errors to help me find bugs, and, when all else failed, to upload the source code and ask it to find the problem.

  This lasted until the first few months of my sophomore spring semester. Though the ai tools I used changed several times(Google Gemini for the most of the time. Gemini has been my first choice till April, 2026.), the workflow remained the same: I did the work myself for the courses that I considered important, and only used ai for assistance. And for courses that I thought was useless but required, I simply relied on ai to quickly finish it so as to avoid wasting too much time. In 2025, despite the huge strides AI has made in programming, it still struggles with mathematics — often falling into circular reasoning or producing false proofs. So I never thought of the possibility that AI could be used to do mathematical homework, which has become a reality now.

### Phase 2

  By the end of April, 2026, that approach was getting more and more unsustainable. That was because of the Software Engineering course. The main goal of the course was to cooperate with team members to create an instant message system. And I together with another classmate was in charge of the front-end part. The language used was TypeScript and the framework was React(The same used by this website.). Before that course, the only web development experience I 'd had was writing some basic html sites. And the course ***DID NOT TAUGHT THOSE THINGS, IT ONLY TAUGHT THE CLASSIC DESIGN ARTS IN THE SOFTWARE ENGINEERING***, which meant that you had to do that yourself. ***BUT I DID NOT HAVE ENOUGH TIME TO LEARN SO MANY THINGS***, starting from html, to css, then to javascript/typescript, and finally, to React. I came to rely more and more heavily on the use of ai, and at last, almost all the code was generated by ai, and what I did in this process was to understand the code written by ai and to make some suggestions for further changes, which made me feel disturbed. After all, it was I that took the class and learned the knowledge, not the ai. I can't in good conscience treat ai's capabilities as my own. Later on I learned that I was not alone - the whole class had turned into a competition of ai, rather than a competition of students' own competences. And the same phenomenon also occur in many other scientific competitions. That's quite nature, since if you do not use ai, you will be surpassed by others easily. But as for me, that is no different from doping in sports. But I had to do it, for I did not had enough time to learn it, and if you did not use it, you would fall behind others soon.

### Phase 3

  Things got even worse. This summer term, I chose a research-oriented course, in the hope of getting an experience of doing research. The subject of the project was exactly Large Language Models. And it lasted 2 weeks. ***THE FIRST TWO LESSONS OF THE COURSE TAUGHT THE BASIC SYNTAX OF PYTHON.*** All the knowledge, including RNN, CNN, and Transformer was taught ***only in less than 1 week***. Then we were required to come up with an idea and complete the project in the remaining 1 week. Believe it or not, the teacher thought that ***IN JUST TWO WEEKS***, we could go from ***COMPLETE PYTHON BEGINNERS*** to ***RESEARCHERS CAPABLE OF INDEPENDENTLY CONDUCTING AI RESEARCH***. Sometimes I will think, the current curricular system is ***ENCOURAGING THE RELIANCE ON AI***.

  Actually the whole work loop ends up looking like this:

  ```mermaid
  flowchart TD
  A[Have no ideas] --> B[Ask AI for some]
  B --> C[Do some check-ups]
  C --> D[Let AI implement the idea you think is promising]
  D --> E{Any problems?}
  E -- Yes --> F[Send the result back to AI for analysis]
  E -- No --> G{Claim strong enough?}
  G -- Yes --> H[end]
  G -- No --> A
  F --> I[AI gives some revision suggestions]
  I --> C
  ```
  
  The **ONLY** thing we do in this process is the verification work. Or a little more, acting as the bridge between AI and the experiment equipment, if you can call that work. The most important and demanding parts in the field of scientific research are simply missing: coming up with innovative ideas and figuring out how to implement them. And  even if it does produce results, I would not consider that mine. This is no different from plagiarism, in my opinion.  
  
## Conclusion
  
  I am not against AI. I use it every day, and I will keep using it. But there is a difference between using a tool and letting it use you. When I let AI write my code, I did not learn to write code. When I let it generate my research ideas, I did not learn to think. The output may look like mine, but the competence behind it is not. And if that competence is never developed, it will not be there when I actually need it.

  And I do hope, in a time when everyone is impatient, we can still find the patience to learn things properly — to struggle with a problem, to sit with not knowing, to earn the understanding we claim to have.
